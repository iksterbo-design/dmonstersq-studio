HOW AIMBOTS WORK — AND WHY THEY GET BANNED

The interesting part, without the ban.

1. The vision pipeline

A modern aimbot doesn't read the game's memory first — it looks at the screen. It grabs frames (10–60 fps), runs a neural net (YOLO/ONNX) to find player-shaped blobs, then computes an angle from screen centre to the target's bounding box.
  • Screen capture — fast framebuffer grab (DXGI Desktop Duplication / X11 capture)
  • Inference — a small CNN, a few ms per frame on a decent GPU
  • Target selection — nearest to crosshair, or "most dangerous"
  • Output — a mouse delta, injected via SendInput / uinput / a driver

2. Why it looks superhuman — and that's the flaw

Real humans have noisy aim. Reaction time is 150–250 ms and varies every time. Human flicks overshoot and correct. An aimbot moves in a clean curve, at a machine-consistent speed, with a variance near zero.

Anti-cheat doesn't need to see the cheat. It measures you.

3. What anti-cheat actually measures

  • Reaction-time distribution — humans form a bell curve. A bot's is a spike at ~0 ms. Instantly suspicious.
  • Input entropy — human mouse paths have jitter. Bot paths are near-perfectly smooth. Low entropy = machine.
  • Angle-to-target correlation — if your crosshair snaps to a head hitbox far more often than chance, the statistics scream.
  • Timing regularity — bots fire on a rhythm. Humans don't.
  • Perfect tracking through walls/occlusion — a bot keeps tracking a target it can't see.
  • Kernel-level telemetry — signed drivers (Vanguard, EAC, BattlEye) see handles, hooks and injected DLLs.

4. The arms race

Cheat devs respond with humanisation: adding jitter, random reaction delays, bezier-curved flicks, and misses. But every humanisation is a statistical fingerprint too — a bot that's "random in exactly the right way" is still not random like a human. This is a genuinely hard adversarial ML problem, and it's why anti-cheat engineers get paid well.

5. The honest conclusion

Aimbots for online multiplayer get you banned, ruin the lobby, and the tech is the exact same computer vision you can build legally for: sports analysis, robotics, accessibility, wildlife cameras, your own game dev, or the aim trainer you're using right now.

Same skills. No ban. Actually employable.
